    23	- Treat lockfile source as evidence. Version strings alone do not prove an advisory is fixed when a registry crate, local patch, fork, or vendored crate may differ.
    24	
    25	## Never
    26	
    27	- Do not assume a stack without evidence.
    28	- Do not make production changes without explicit approval.
    29	- Do not expose secret values in output.
    30	- Do not recommend autonomous high-risk remediation.
    31	- Do not claim a project is safe because it “works locally.”
    32	- Do not bury P0 findings under nice-to-have improvements.
    33	- Do not present the first approval batch as the full fix scope.
    34	- Do not overwhelm beginners with every possible enterprise control at once.
    35	- Do not shame the user or turn findings into a performance.
    36	- Do not aim jokes at the user, their skill, or their intelligence.
    37	- Do not load or paste advanced references unless needed.
    38	- Do not generate the HTML dashboard unless the user asks for it.
    39	- Do not force English-only output when the user or codebase is clearly working in another language.
    40	- Do not assume the second language from region or codebase hints alone; ask the user to confirm the inferred candidate second language first.
    41	- Do not claim a dependency alert is resolved from a local branch, PR branch, or local build alone.
    42	- Do not broad-search build output, dependency folders, generated files, or vendored code unless that path is the explicit subject.
    43	- Do not claim all public repositories are clean without exact namespace, repository count, timestamp, fork policy, and current evidence.
    44	
    45	## Token efficiency and context loading
    46	
    47	- Read the smallest useful context first.
    48	- Keep broad sweeps compact.
    49	- Use tables for the complete findings/backlog.
    50	- Use stable finding IDs and compact evidence references instead of repeating detail.
    51	- Expand details only for blockers, high-risk findings, and the next approval batch.
    52	- Summarize evidence; do not dump raw files, logs, source, or standards excerpts into reports.
    53	- Do not load the entire advanced library unless a finding needs it.
    54	- Dashboard output is opt-in; do not generate the HTML dashboard unless the user asks.
    55	- If the user does not want HTML, provide the inline Markdown dashboard fallback instead of another visual file.
    56	- Keep the audit complete even when the initial explanation is compact.
    57	- Exclude `target/`, `node_modules/`, `vendor/`, generated schemas, and build artifacts from broad scans and searches unless they are the affected surface.
    58	
    59	## Decision priority
    60	
    61	1. User safety and data protection.
    62	2. Production correctness and reversibility.
    63	3. Complete risk visibility.
    64	4. Clear explanation.
    65	5. Small useful fixes.
    66	6. Learning value.
    67	7. Optimization.
     1	# CheckYourself Coverage Matrix
     2	
     3	A CheckYourself diagnostic is not allowed to stop after a few obvious issues. The reviewer must sweep every relevant production surface below and mark each one as one of:
     4	
     5	- **Pass** — evidence shows the control exists and is credible.
     6	- **Finding** — evidence shows a gap or risky implementation.
     7	- **Unknown** — the reviewer cannot tell from available evidence. Unknowns in critical areas should become questions or temporary risks.
     8	- **Not applicable** — the app genuinely does not use this surface.
     9	
    10	The goal is maximum practical coverage, not fake certainty. If the AI cannot inspect enough of the project, it must say what it cannot verify.
    11	
    12	| # | Surface | What to inspect | Typical findings |
    13	|---:|---|---|---|
    14	| 1 | Product purpose and users | What the app does, who can use it, who can be harmed | unclear risk model, missing admin/user distinction |
    15	| 2 | Stack and architecture | framework, backend, database, host, integrations | unsupported assumptions, hidden serverless limits |
    16	| 3 | Frontend UX and client safety | routing, forms, validation, state, accessibility, error states | client-only protection, broken loading/error flows |
    17	| 4 | API and backend services | routes, input validation, business rules, uploads, webhooks | unsafe writes, missing validation, webhook trust bugs |
    18	| 5 | Auth and permissions | login, sessions, roles, admin paths, server-side authorization | auth only in UI, role bypass, weak session handling |
    19	| 6 | Data storage and migrations | schema, migrations, backups, constraints, seed data | destructive migrations, no constraints, no backup evidence |
    20	| 7 | User/tenant isolation | tenant/user filters, RLS, cache keys, exports, logs | cross-user data leaks, tenant-blind cache keys |
    21	| 8 | Secrets and environment config | env files, secret handling, public variables, config drift | exposed keys, missing env examples, prod/dev mismatch |
    22	| 9 | Security and threat model | OWASP-style risks, injection, SSRF, XSS, CSRF, dependencies | untrusted input reaches dangerous sinks |
    23	| 10 | Privacy and data governance | PII, retention, deletion, consent, logs, third-party sharing | collecting sensitive data without policy or deletion path |
    24	| 11 | Tests and quality gates | unit/integration/e2e tests, dangerous paths, CI checks | no tests around auth, payments, writes, or data isolation |
    25	| 12 | CI/CD and supply chain | lockfiles, dependency scans, SBOM/provenance, branch rules | unpinned deps, no scan, manual deploy risk |
    26	| 13 | Hosting, deployment, rollback | environments, deploy process, preview/prod split, rollback | no rollback plan, manual env drift, prod secrets in dev |
    27	| 14 | Cloud infrastructure/IaC | Terraform/Pulumi/cloud config, networking, storage permissions | public buckets, wide IAM, no infrastructure review |
    28	| 15 | Performance, caching, rate limits | latency, load, indexes, cache correctness, abuse throttling | no rate limits, N+1 queries, cache leakage |
    29	| 16 | Scaling and resilience | queues, retries, circuit breakers, graceful degradation | single point of failure, retry storms, no backpressure |
    30	| 17 | Observability and incident response | logs, metrics, traces, error tracking, alerts, runbooks | no useful logs, no owner, no incident path |
    31	| 18 | Availability and recovery | backups, restore tests, disaster recovery, data export | backups untested, no RTO/RPO, no recovery drill |
    32	| 19 | AI/RAG/agent governance | prompts, tool permissions, evals, citations, refusal, sandboxing | hallucinated answers, unsafe tools, prompt injection, no evals |
    33	| 20 | Learning needs | gaps that reveal what the builder should learn next | generic learning plan not tied to real findings |

    35	## Completeness rule
    36	
    37	The report must include a **Coverage Sweep** table. Every row above must be represented as Pass, Finding, Unknown, or Not applicable. The remediation backlog must include every row marked Finding and any high-impact Unknown that blocks confidence.
    38	
    39	In prose, use **Not applicable**. In the coverage JSON written for the CLI, use
    40	exactly these status values: `Pass`, `Finding`, `Unknown`, `NotApplicable`.
     1	# Scoring Method
     2	
     3	The Production Reality Score is a 0–100 readiness confidence score.
     4	
     5	It is not a moral judgment and not a guarantee. It is an evidence-based estimate of how ready the app is for real users.
     6	
     7	## Category weights
     8	
     9	| Category | Weight |
    10	|---|---:|
    11	| Data, privacy, and tenant/user isolation | 18 |
    12	| Auth, permissions, and session safety | 14 |
    13	| Secrets, environment, and runtime config | 10 |
    14	| API, validation, uploads, and business logic | 10 |
    15	| Testing and quality gates | 10 |
    16	| Deployment, release, rollback, and CI/CD | 8 |
    17	| Observability, logs, errors, and incident response | 8 |
    18	| Performance, scaling, caching, and rate limits | 8 |
    19	| Frontend UX, accessibility, and client safety | 8 |
    20	| AI/RAG/agent governance, if applicable | 6 |
    21	
    22	If a category does not apply, mark it `NotApplicable` and provide an explicit `not_applicable_reason`. Its weight slot is not redistributed; the CLI awards the full weight for that category automatically when a valid reason is present. Do not adjust other category weights. Hand-scored totals must treat a NotApplicable category as if it received full credit for that weight, matching the behavior of `score --findings --coverage`.
    23	
    24	In prose, write **Not applicable**. In coverage JSON, use the exact enum
    25	`NotApplicable`; both spellings describe the same state.
    26	
    27	## Caps
    28	
    29	- Any unresolved P0 caps the final score at 49.
    30	- Any unresolved P1 caps the final score at 74.
    31	- Missing evidence in a critical category caps the final score at 84. This cap applies in every score mode, including estimates.
    32	- A score above 90 requires credible evidence for tests, secrets handling, deployment/rollback, observability, auth, and data boundaries. This cap applies in every score mode, including estimates.
    33	
    34	## Executable scoring contract
    35	
    36	The CLI computes the base score and caps deterministically:
    37	
    38	1. For each category, start with its weight.
    39	2. Subtract the evidence penalty for an `Unknown` category (`100%` of the
    40	   weight for critical categories C1/C2/C3, otherwise `50%`). Subtract each
    41	   unresolved finding penalty using `P0 = 100%`, `P1 = 60%`, `P2 = 25%`, or
    42	   `P3 = 10%` of that category's weight. Clamp each category award to
    43	   `[0, weight]`.
    44	3. `base_score = round(sum(category_award))`.
    45	4. `minimum_cap = min(100, 49 if unresolved P0, 74 if unresolved P1, 84 if
    46	   critical evidence is missing, 90 if a high-score launch-gate category lacks
    47	   evidence)`.
    48	5. `final_score = min(base_score, minimum_cap)`.
    49	
    50	`NotApplicable` with a concrete reason retains the category's full weight. The
    51	executable reference is [`docs/cli.md#scoring`](../docs/cli.md#scoring), backed
    52	by [`tools/checkyourself.py`](../tools/checkyourself.py).
    53	
    54	## Confidence labels
    55	
    56	Every score must include a confidence label:
    57	
    58	- **High confidence** — direct repo/config/test evidence.
    59	- **Medium confidence** — enough evidence, but some assumptions.
    60	- **Low confidence** — limited context; score may change significantly.
    61	
    62	## Required explanation
    63	
    64	For each category, say:
    65	
    66	- what evidence was found;
    67	- what was missing;
    68	- what points were awarded;
    69	- what would raise the score.

    37	## What is CheckYourself?
    38	
    39	CheckYourself is an open-source **production-readiness audit system** — a structured, staged engineering framework of context files, scoring logic, output schemas, templates, and a deep production-hardening capability stack that you load as an AI coding assistant's operating context, so it can grade an AI-built app the way real production would: honestly, completely, and before launch.
    40	
    41	It answers one question that matters to every "vibe coder," indie hacker, and AI-assisted builder: **"Is this app actually ready to ship, and if not, what exactly is wrong and how do I fix it?"**
    42	
    43	Unlike a "top three issues" linter, CheckYourself builds a **complete findings register** and a **complete remediation backlog**, scores production readiness from 0–100, and walks you through fixes one safe, reversible batch at a time. When the audit is done, it generates a **bespoke learning plan** so you actually learn from what your project was missing.
    44	
    45	It is also organized as an ICM-style context workspace: [`CONTEXT.md`](CONTEXT.md) routes the agent to staged folders, each major stage has its own `CONTEXT.md`, and durable handoff artifacts belong in stage `output/` folders. CheckYourself is not affiliated with the RinDig ICM project; it uses the same file-first idea so agents know what to read, do, and produce at each step.
    46

    50	
    51	Apps built fast with AI tools tend to look finished long before they are safe to launch. The gaps are usually invisible from the happy path: missing auth checks, unvalidated inputs, leaked secrets, no backups, no rollback, no tests, no rate limits, no error tracking.
    52	
    53	CheckYourself gives you reality **before production does the grading** — a calm, complete, plain-English second pass that any AI assistant can run on your behalf.

   266	Yes. CheckYourself starts **read-only** by default. It will not change code or configuration until you approve a specific, reversible fix, and it re-verifies after every batch.
   267	
   268	### How is it different from a linter or a "top issues" tool?
   269	A linter flags style and a few obvious problems. CheckYourself builds a *complete* findings register and remediation backlog across the entire production surface — auth, data, secrets, CI/CD, deployment, observability, privacy, and more — then guides fixes and produces a learning plan.
   270	
   271	### What does the Production Reality Score mean?
   272	It is a 0–100 production-readiness score with severity caps and explicit reasoning, explained in [`docs/checkyourself-score-explained.md`](docs/checkyourself-score-explained.md). A low score with clear findings is more useful than a falsely high one.

     6	# CheckYourself
     7	
     8	## Overview
     9	
    10	Use CheckYourself to turn an AI assistant into a calm, evidence-first production reviewer. Start read-only, inspect the whole relevant launch surface, produce a scored Production Reality Report, and ask for approval before changing code.
    11	
    12	## Workflow
    13	
    14	1. Identify the project and available CheckYourself context.
    15	   - If this repository or a copied `checkyourself` folder is present, start with `CONTEXT.md`, `AGENTS.md`, `rules.md`, `02_RUN_DIAGNOSTIC/coverage-matrix.md`, and `02_RUN_DIAGNOSTIC/scoring-method.md`.
    16	   - If only this skill is available, use the workflow below and ask for the smallest missing project evidence.
    17	   - Infer stack, audience, data shape, deploy target, and risk level from files and configuration. Label guesses.
    18	
    19	2. Run deterministic checks when safe and available.
    20	   - Prefer read-only commands.
    21	   - Each scan finding carries a stable, semantic rule ID (for example
    22	     `CY-SECRET-001`, `CY-CONFIG-001`) that stays the same across runs, so you
    23	     can suppress, diff, and cite findings reliably.
    24	   - If `tools/checkyourself.py` exists, the deterministic pipeline is:
    25	
    26	```bash
    27	python3 tools/checkyourself.py describe --format json
    28	python3 tools/checkyourself.py scan /path/to/project --deep --format json --no-write
    29	python3 tools/checkyourself.py coverage --emit            # fill with evidence, then:
    30	python3 tools/checkyourself.py score --findings scan.json --coverage coverage.json --format json
    31	python3 tools/checkyourself.py backlog --findings scan.json --format json
    32	python3 tools/checkyourself.py next --findings scan.json --format json
    33	python3 tools/checkyourself.py diff --old baseline.json --new current.json --ci   # regression gate
    34	```
    35	
    36	   - Treat scanner findings as confirmed evidence. Do not invent a separate
    37	     scoring or backlog path. If Python is unavailable, sweep manually and say
    38	     the score is hand-computed.
    39	
    40	### Manual fallback contract
    41	
    42	When the CLI is unavailable, manual output still uses a canonical rule-ID
    43	registry and evidence rubric:
    44	
    45	| Rule ID | Canonical manual condition |
    46	|---|---|
    47	| `CY-MANUAL-AUTH-001` | Auth, permission, session, or admin behavior lacks verified server-side evidence. |
    48	| `CY-MANUAL-DATA-001` | Data storage, recovery, retention, or tenant isolation lacks verified evidence. |
    49	| `CY-MANUAL-PRIVACY-001` | Privacy, consent, deletion, or third-party data handling lacks verified evidence. |
    50	| `CY-MANUAL-TEST-001` | A dangerous or launch-critical path lacks a focused test receipt. |
    51	| `CY-MANUAL-RELEASE-001` | Deployment, rollback, CI/CD, or supply-chain behavior lacks a verified receipt. |
    52	| `CY-MANUAL-OBS-001` | Observability, alerting, or incident response lacks a verified receipt. |
    53	| `CY-MANUAL-AI-001` | AI/RAG/agent permissions, evaluation, or refusal behavior lacks verified evidence. |
    54	| `CY-MANUAL-OTHER-001` | A material gap does not match another registered manual condition. |
    55	
    56	Reuse a detector ID from the [canonical detector-rule registry](../../docs/cli.md#canonical-detector-rule-registry)
    57	when the condition matches a shipped detector. Do not invent a new ID or
    58	renumber this registry for one report.
    59	
    60	Every manual finding must include: the registry ID; severity and category; an
    61	exact file, command output, or owner-provided artifact with date/scope; the
    62	plain-English risk; and a status. A `Pass` needs direct evidence, a `Finding`
    63	needs evidence of the gap and its harm, `Unknown` needs an explicit missing-
    64	evidence request, and `Not applicable` needs a concrete reason. If no artifact
    65	can be inspected, label the result as a low-confidence hand-computed estimate.
    66	
    67	### Deterministic score contract
    68	
    69	The executable score is `final_score = min(base_score, minimum_cap)`, where
    70	`base_score` is the rounded sum of clamped per-category awards after evidence
    71	and unresolved-finding penalties. The cap is the minimum of 100, 49 for an
    72	unresolved P0, 74 for an unresolved P1, 84 for a missing critical-evidence
    73	category, and 90 for a missing high-score launch-gate category. `NotApplicable`
    74	with a concrete reason retains its category weight. See the [CLI scoring
    75	contract](../../docs/cli.md#scoring) and [executable implementation](../../tools/checkyourself.py).
    76	
    77	3. Sweep the production surface.
    78	   Cover product purpose, frontend UX, accessibility, backend/API behavior, auth, data storage, migrations, secrets, runtime config, tests, CI/CD, dependencies, deploy/rollback, observability, performance, privacy, compliance, and AI/RAG/agent governance when relevant.
    79	
    80	4. Produce a Production Reality Report.
    81	   Include:
    82	   - executive summary;
    83	   - what the app appears to do;
    84	   - detected stack and confidence;
    85	   - unknowns and assumptions;
    86	   - Production Reality Score from 0 to 100 with severity caps explained
    87	     (P0 caps at 49, P1 at 74, missing critical evidence at 84, missing
    88	     launch-gate evidence at 90; the evidence caps apply even to estimates,
    89	     and absence of findings is treated as Unknown, never an automatic Pass);
    90	   - coverage sweep marked Pass, Finding, Unknown, or Not applicable;
    91	   - P0/P1/P2/P3 findings;
    92	   - evidence table;
    93	   - complete ranked remediation backlog;
    94	   - safest first approval batch;
    95	   - questions that would change the diagnosis;
    96	   - learning-plan seeds based on the actual gaps.
    97	
    98	5. Recommend before acting.
    99	   For each backlog item include finding ID, severity, fix summary, impact or blast radius, why it matters, likely files/systems touched, verification, rollback idea, learning value, and status. Do not modify files until the user approves a specific fix or batch.
   100	
   101	6. After approval, run the guided fix loop.
   102	   Make the smallest reversible change, verify it, update finding status, rescore when evidence changes, and continue until findings are fixed, accepted as risk, deferred with a reason, suppressed with evidence, or proven not applicable.
   103	
   104	7. Create the learning plan.
   105	   Tie lessons to the real findings and fixes. Include what to learn next, why it matters, a 7-day plan, a 30-day plan, small exercises inside the app, and what to ignore for now.

     1	# Retrofit Process Learnings — 2026-09-04
     2	
     3	This note records process lessons from the documentation, validation, and
     4	evidence close-out. It is for maintainers running future multi-worker changes;
     5	it does not change the product contract.
     6	
     7	## Evidence freshness has two clocks
     8	
     9	A worker can observe a correct local projection while the orchestrator's branch
    10	still lacks that projection. The reverse also happens: a commit can exist while
    11	generated receipts, screenshots, or reports still describe an older tree. A
    12	"code-complete" report is therefore not proof that the committed or released
    13	surface is current.
    14	
    15	Keep these states separate in every wave:
    16	
    17	1. worker worktree state;
    18	2. orchestrator branch state;
    19	3. default-branch or release state;
    20	4. generated proof state.
    21	
    22	Record the source state used for each receipt, regenerate derived proof from one
    23	captured evidence set, and re-run acceptance after integration. Treat a report
    24	that projects a future commit as a plan, not as current proof.
    25	
    26	## Public validation needs an artifact boundary
    27	
    28	Retrofit reports are process evidence, not public product documentation. When a
    29	public Markdown/link validator walks `_retrofit-*` artifacts, intentionally
    30	incomplete evidence links can make the public gate fail and hide whether the
    31	product surface itself is healthy. This is a boundary-design failure, not a
    32	reason to erase useful evidence.
    33	
    34	Choose one explicit policy before the next retrofit: exclude process-artifact
    35	directories from public validation, or validate them with a separate historical
    36	artifact contract. Keep the exclusion narrow and visible, and report both
    37	product-surface results and artifact-contract results separately.
    38	
    39	## License files and badges are different evidence classes
    40	
    41	A README badge is a presentation claim. The LICENSE file is the legal artifact;
    42	the manifest and NOTICE provide machine-readable and attribution context. A
    43	green badge cannot override a conflicting license file. Resolve the owner's
    44	legal choice first, then align the badge, prose, manifest, LICENSE, and NOTICE
    45	as one change and validate every public claim.
    46	
    47	## Capture the baseline before changing a wave
    48	
    49	The required validator and full test suite repeatedly exposed inherited README
    50	and retrofit-artifact failures only after a worker had changed code. Without a
    51	pre-wave receipt, an acceptance failure is ambiguous: regression, inherited
    52	debt, or an artifact owned by another lane.
    53	
    54	Before implementation, capture the exact commands, exit codes, failure list,
    55	and ownership of known failures. After implementation, compare the same checks
    56	with the same scope. A wave passes only when it introduces no new failure; an
    57	inherited failure remains visible and explicitly out of scope.
    58	
    59	## Orchestrator and worker Git ownership must stay explicit
    60	
    61	Workers need a precise changed-file list and verification receipt, but the
    62	orchestrator owns branch integration and commits. A worker's local clean status
    63	or commit is not a merged delivery receipt, and an orchestrator's integration
    64	commit does not replace worker-level proof.
    65	
    66	The dispatch brief should name the owner of branch, commit, push, and release
    67	actions. The worker report should list every changed file and the exact checks
    68	run. The orchestrator should re-check the integrated tree before reporting
    69	completion. Keep task-tracker setup in the worker harness ready before dispatch;
    70	otherwise even status bookkeeping becomes an avoidable failure mode.
    71	
    72	## Generated receipts should have one reproducible producer
    73	
    74	Manual date and scan-count edits created avoidable freshness drift during the
    75	close-out. A dependency-free receipt command should capture scan, coverage,
    76	score, and rendering inputs once, then generate all derived proof from that
    77	same evidence. Until such a command exists, label hand-refreshed receipts with
    78	their source date, scope, and known proof gaps.

   166	## How it works
   167	
   168	CheckYourself runs as a staged workflow, each stage with its own context file so your AI tool always knows what to read, do, and produce:
   169	
   170	1. **Project context** — the agent maps what your app does and detects the stack.
   171	2. **Run diagnostic** — a read-only sweep produces the Production Reality Report and score.
   172	3. **Guided fix mode** — you approve fixes in safe batches; the agent applies and verifies them.
   173	4. **Learning plan** — the agent writes a plan from the real gaps it found.
   174	5. **Dashboard (optional)** — a self-contained HTML or inline Markdown view of everything.
   175	
   176	Each stage is defined by its own context files, scoring rules, schemas, and templates — so the agent always knows what to read, what to do, and what to produce. The advanced engine in [`90_ADVANCED/`](90_ADVANCED/) deepens any stage when a domain warrants it.

     2

     1	# CheckYourself Score Explained
     2	
     3	A score is useful because people need a simple signal.
     4	
     5	A score is dangerous because people may treat it as permission to ship.
     6	
     7	So CheckYourself uses the score as a conversation starter, not as a guarantee.
     8	
     9	## What the score means
    10	
    11	The score estimates production-readiness confidence based on available evidence.
    12	
    13	It is deliberately hard to game. Absence of findings is treated as absence of
    14	evidence, not proof of safety: if the scanner finds no secrets, the secrets
    15	surface is `Unknown`, not `Pass`. A coverage surface marked `Pass` without
    16	evidence, or `Not applicable` without a reason, is downgraded to `Unknown`, and
    17	any required surface omitted from the coverage artifact counts as `Unknown`. So
    18	omitting or hand-waving a surface never scores better than honestly reporting it.
    19	
    20	## Score modes and confidence
    21	
    22	- **Coverage-backed** — you supplied a filled coverage artifact. Only this mode
    23	  can reach `high` confidence, and only when all 20 surfaces are present with
    24	  real evidence.
    25	- **Scan-derived estimate** — derived from a scan with no coverage. Confidence
    26	  stays `low`.
    27	- **Finding-only estimate** — derived from a bare findings list. Confidence
    28	  stays `low`.
    29	
    30	The evidence caps below apply in **every** mode, so an estimate can never report
    31	a launch-ready number.
    32	
    33	## What the score does not mean
    34	
    35	It does not mean:
    36	
    37	- the app is secure;
    38	- the app is compliant;
    39	- the app has been penetration tested;
    40	- a human expert reviewed it;
    41	- production launch is risk-free.
    42	
    43	## Why caps matter
    44	
    45	Caps stop polish from hiding risk. They apply in order, and the lowest one wins:
    46	
    47	- an unresolved **P0** caps the score at **49**;
    48	- an unresolved **P1** caps the score at **74**;
    49	- **missing evidence in a critical category** (data, auth, secrets) caps at **84**;
    50	- **missing key launch-gate evidence** (tests, secrets, deploy/rollback,
    51	  observability, auth, data boundaries) caps at **90**.
    52	
    53	A beautiful app that leaks user data is not production-ready, so a single P0
    54	keeps the score at 49 no matter how polished everything else looks.
    55	
    56	## How to raise the score honestly
    57	
    58	- Fix P0/P1 issues.
    59	- Add tests for risky paths.
    60	- Document deployment and rollback.
    61	- Prove secrets are not hardcoded.
    62	- Add observability.
    63	- Verify auth and data boundaries.
    64	- Fill the coverage matrix with real evidence so the score is coverage-backed.
    65	- Re-run the diagnostic, and use `diff` to confirm you fixed risk without adding new.

    42	# spec-driven-delivery
    43	Convert ambiguous product intent into machine-checkable specs, acceptance criteria, and change plans.
    44	## Operating contract
    45	
    46	Act as a production hardening specialist for **01 Product Intent & Contracts**. Use model-agnostic reasoning: no instruction, output, or workflow in this capability depends on a particular model vendor or agent runtime. Prefer deterministic evidence over persuasive prose. When evidence is missing, name the assumption and make it visible in the output.
    47	## When to activate
    48	Use this capability when defining, refining, or reviewing a feature before implementation. Trigger on requirements, user stories, acceptance criteria, OpenAPI, AsyncAPI, JSON Schema, contracts, ADRs, RFCs, roadmap items, or “turn this idea into buildable work”.
    49	## Inputs to request or inspect
    50	- product idea
    51	- research note
    52	- customer feedback
    53	- existing APIs
    54	- schema definitions
    55	- constraints
    56	
    57	## Work protocol
    58	1. Separate intent from implementation. Capture actors, jobs, constraints, non-goals, and success measures first.
    59	2. Write contracts before code: request/response shapes, events, permissions, data lifecycle, observability signals, and failure modes.
    60	3. Define acceptance criteria as verifiable statements, not vague outcomes.
    61	4. Identify product, technical, security, privacy, and operational assumptions. Mark blocking assumptions explicitly.
    62	5. Create a minimum viable production slice: the smallest implementation that includes auth, tests, telemetry, rollback, and docs.
    63	6. Update specs when requirements change; regenerate or refactor code from the source of truth rather than letting code drift silently.
    64	
    65	## Required output format
    66	
    67	Return a concise report with these sections unless the user requested a concrete file or code diff:
    68	
    69	1. **Scope interpreted** — what is in and out.
    70	2. **Findings / decisions** — ordered by production risk, not by discovery order.
    71	3. **Recommended actions** — owner-ready tasks with priority and rationale.
    72	4. **Verification evidence** — tests, scans, contracts, telemetry, commands, or review steps required.
    73	5. **Residual risk / assumptions** — what remains uncertain and how to resolve it.
    74	6. **Hand-offs** — other capabilities that should review the work.
    75	## Verification gates
    76	- Every feature has a clear owner, measurable acceptance criteria, and a non-goal section.
    77	- Every external boundary has a schema or contract and compatibility strategy.
    78	- Every production behavior names expected failure modes and user-visible fallback behavior.
    79	- Human review checkpoints exist for priorities, specs, and intent-to-ship decisions.
    80	
    81	## Anti-patterns to block
    82	- Do not start with framework scaffolding when the contract is unknown.
    83	- Do not let generated code become the only specification.
    84	- Do not accept “works locally” as an acceptance criterion.
    85	
    86	## Hand-off rules
    87	- Hand off to the orchestrator when a request spans more than three production layers or has unclear risk ownership.
    88	- Consider `prodhardening.api_backend_services` when its layer is implicated by the findings.
    89	- Consider `prodhardening.testing_quality_engineering` when its layer is implicated by the findings.
    90	- Consider `prodhardening.orchestrator` when its layer is implicated by the findings.
    91	
    92	## Examples
    93	**Prompt:** “Turn this feature idea into an implementation plan.”
    94	
    95	**Expected handling:** Return a spec, acceptance tests, API/data contracts, risk assumptions, and sequencing.
    96	
    97	**Prompt:** “Review this OpenAPI file before implementation.”
    98	
    99	**Expected handling:** Check completeness, consistency, auth, error model, pagination, idempotency, and testability.
   100	
   101	## References to load on demand
   102	- `../../references/spec-driven-delivery.md` — read when detailed checklists, templates, or implementation guidance are needed.
   103	- `../../templates/adr.md` — read when detailed checklists, templates, or implementation guidance are needed.
   104	- `../../templates/spec-template.md` — read when detailed checklists, templates, or implementation guidance are needed.
   105	
   106	## Completion definition
   107	The work is complete only when recommendations are actionable, verification steps are explicit, and unresolved assumptions are visible. Never present a system as production-ready solely because code was generated or a checklist was copied.

## 5. The missing capability: challenge a completion claim

### #8 — SEV-2: The core interface is not bound to what the user originally asked to accomplish

The advanced specification guidance already requires contracts before code and measurable acceptance criteria. **The 10× capability is a verifier-owned challenge runner.** Freeze the accepted completion claim, derive a falsifiable challenge, and execute it against the captured source state using checks the builder cannot silently rewrite. Record what behavior the challenge establishes and what remains unproven; invalidate that receipt when relevant inputs change.

**Fix direction:** Add claim-specific challenge execution that binds accepted task outcomes to independently captured results and explicit invalidation conditions.
